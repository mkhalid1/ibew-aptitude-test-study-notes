# Formulas and substitution

**In one line:** you are given a formula, you put numbers in for the letters, and you work out the answer without a calculator. Some items turn it around and ask you to rearrange the formula.

This is part of the **Algebra and Functions** test (33 items, 46 minutes). The official sample page from the electrical training ALLIANCE opens with an item of this kind, which tells you how central it is.

## What it measures

Whether you can follow a rule exactly. An electrician works from formulas every day: load calculations, voltage drop, conduit fill. The test does not expect you to know any of those formulas. It gives you the formula and checks that you can:

- replace each letter with the right number
- follow the order of operations without slipping
- handle negative numbers and squares correctly
- rearrange a formula to get a different letter on its own

The electrical training ALLIANCE says you do not need to memorise formulas and that no electrical knowledge is required. Everything you need is in the question.

## How the questions look

A formula, the values of some letters, and four answer choices, labelled A to D. Only one answer is correct.

The two main shapes:

| Shape | What it asks | Example in our own words |
|---|---|---|
| **Evaluate** | Find the value of one letter when the others are given | "If m = 3 and n = 8, what is T?" |
| **Rearrange** | Which formula gives a different letter on its own | "Which formula gives I by itself?" |

A third shape shows up less often: **what happens if**. For example, what happens to the result if one value doubles.

## A method that works

1. **Copy the formula onto scratch paper.** Do not work in your head. Most errors here are copying errors, not maths errors.
2. **Write each value in brackets as you substitute.** If n = −4, write 3(−4), not 3 − 4. Brackets stop sign errors.
3. **Work inside brackets first, then powers, then multiply and divide, then add and subtract.** Left to right within each step.
4. **Check the answer choices against your result.** If yours is not there, look for a sign or bracket mistake before you guess.
5. **For rearranging, undo the operations in reverse order.** Whatever was done to the letter last, undo first. Then check by putting simple numbers into both versions.

## Traps that cost points

- **Squaring a negative.** (−4)² is 16, not −16. Write the brackets and you will not fall for it.
- **Subtracting a negative.** 5 − (−3) is 8. When a value you substitute is negative and the formula has a minus in front of it, the two minuses make a plus.
- **Squaring before subtracting.** (a − b)² means subtract first, then square. a² − b² is a different thing and often gives one of the wrong choices.
- **Dividing only part of the top.** In (a + b) ÷ c, the whole of a + b is divided. A fraction bar works like a bracket.
- **Rearranging one side only.** Whatever you do to one side of the equals sign, you do to the other.
- **Picking the first choice that looks close.** The wrong answers are built from the most common mistakes. A close match is not a check.

## Timing

The Algebra and Functions test gives you 46 minutes for 33 items, a little under 84 seconds each. Most substitution items take 30 to 60 seconds once the method is automatic, which buys time for harder items later. The electrical training ALLIANCE says most applicants have plenty of time if they work steadily, and advises not spending too long on any one question. If you are stuck after about two minutes, mark your best choice and move on.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the NJATC aptitude test or any publisher's materials. Work without a calculator, as on the real test.

**Item 1.** The perimeter of a rectangle is P = 2(L + W). If L = 9 and W = 4, what is P?

A. 22 · B. 26 · C. 36 · D. 13

**Item 2.** Consider the formula R = x² − 3x. If x = −4, what is R?

A. −4 · B. 4 · C. 28 · D. −28

**Item 3.** Consider the formula A = (h ÷ 2)(b + c). Which formula gives h by itself?

A. h = 2A ÷ (b + c) · B. h = A ÷ (2(b + c)) · C. h = 2A − b − c · D. h = (b + c) ÷ 2A

**Item 4.** Consider the formula W = 3k + 2j. If W = 26 and k = 6, what is j?

A. 4 · B. 8 · C. 3 · D. 16

**Item 5.** Consider the formula E = m × v² ÷ 2. If v doubles and m stays the same, what happens to E?

A. E doubles · B. E becomes four times as large · C. E halves · D. E does not change

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 5</summary>

**Item 1: B (26).** Add inside the brackets first: 9 + 4 = 13. Then multiply: 2 × 13 = 26. *Why not A:* 22 comes from 2 × 9 + 4, which forgets that the 2 multiplies both lengths. *Why not D:* 13 stops before the multiplication.

**Item 2: C (28).** Write the brackets: (−4)² − 3(−4). (−4)² = 16. 3 × (−4) = −12, and subtracting −12 adds 12. So 16 + 12 = 28. *Why not A:* −4 comes from −16 + 12, which squares the negative wrongly. *Why not B:* 4 comes from 16 − 12, which forgets that subtracting a negative adds.

**Item 3: A (h = 2A ÷ (b + c)).** h is divided by 2, then multiplied by (b + c). Undo the last step first: divide both sides by (b + c) to get A ÷ (b + c) = h ÷ 2. Then multiply both sides by 2. Check: if h = 4, b = 1, c = 2, then A = 2 × 3 = 6, and 2 × 6 ÷ 3 = 4. *Why not B:* that divides by 2 where it should multiply.

**Item 4: A (4).** Put in what you know: 26 = 3 × 6 + 2j, so 26 = 18 + 2j. Subtract 18: 8 = 2j. Divide by 2: j = 4. *Why not B:* 8 stops before dividing by 2.

**Item 5: B (four times as large).** v is squared. If v doubles, v² becomes (2v)² = 4v², so E is four times as large. Check with numbers: m = 2, v = 1 gives E = 1. m = 2, v = 2 gives E = 4. *Why not A:* doubling would be true only if v were not squared.

</details>

- **5 of 5:** substitution is safe for you. Keep it fast and spend your time on [number patterns](number-patterns.md) or [functions and inequalities](functions-tables-and-inequalities.md).
