# Reading comprehension

**In one line:** you read a passage and answer questions about what it says, what it means, and what can be concluded from it.

This is the second test in the battery: **Reading Comprehension, 36 items in 51 minutes**. The electrical training ALLIANCE describes it as a test of your ability to obtain information from written passages, and notes that the sample passage it publishes is shorter than those on the actual test. Plenty of applicants spend all their time on algebra and treat reading as the easy half. It is half of the battery, so it deserves real practice.

## What it measures

Whether you can pull exact information out of unfamiliar text and reason from it, without adding what you already know. Apprentices read code books, installation instructions, safety procedures and job specifications. Misreading one word in any of them causes real problems.

The questions fall into five types:

| Type | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Detail** | What the passage states about a specific point |
| **Main idea** | What the passage is mostly about, or why it was written |
| **Inference** | What follows from the passage, though it is not stated outright |
| **Vocabulary in context** | What a word means as it is used in the passage |
| **Application** | What would probably happen in a new situation, using the passage's logic |

The official sample includes an application item: it asks what would probably have happened under a different condition, using the logic the passage sets out. Those are the items people find hardest, and the method below handles them.

## How the questions look

A passage, usually several paragraphs, followed by several multiple-choice questions with four choices labelled A to D. Each question has one correct answer. Passages can be about almost anything: history, science, a workplace notice, a technical process. You do not need any background knowledge. The passage contains everything you need.

## A method that works

1. **Skim the passage once for its shape.** What is it about, and how is it organised: a story in time order, a process in steps, a set of rules with exceptions?
2. **Read the question and decide its type.** A detail question sends you back to one sentence. An inference question asks you to connect two.
3. **Find the line.** For every answer, put your finger on the sentence that supports it. If you cannot point to a line, do not choose that answer.
4. **Eliminate, then choose.** Cross out answers that contradict the passage, go further than it does, or answer a different question. Usually two choices go quickly.
5. **For application questions,** state the passage's rule in one sentence first ("a missed class must be made up within two weeks, unless the job ran late"), then apply it to the new case.
6. **For vocabulary questions,** put each choice into the sentence in place of the word. The right one keeps the sentence's meaning.

## Traps that cost points

- **Choosing what is true in real life rather than in the passage.** The answer must come from the text, even when you know more about the topic.
- **Extreme words.** "All", "always", "never", "only" and "at once" make a choice harder to support. Check them carefully.
- **The right detail for the wrong question.** A choice can repeat words from the passage and still not answer what was asked.
- **Rules with exceptions.** Notices and procedures often state a rule and then an exception with conditions. Both conditions must be met.
- **Numbers that look familiar.** If a passage mentions 214 households and 6 percent, a choice may use the right number for the wrong fact.
- **Overreading inference questions.** The correct inference is the smallest step from the text, not the most interesting one.

## Timing

51 minutes for 36 items is about 85 seconds per item, and that includes reading time. A useful split: spend two to three minutes reading each passage once, then 45 to 60 seconds per question. Do not reread the whole passage for each question; go straight to the line you need. The electrical training ALLIANCE says most applicants have plenty of time if they work steadily, so a calm, even pace is better than rushing early.

## Practice items

This passage and its 4 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from the NJATC aptitude test or any publisher's materials.

### Passage 1

> A lightning rod does not attract lightning from far away, and it does not prevent a strike. Its job is to give a strike that is already going to hit a building an easy path to the ground.
>
> A typical system has three parts: metal rods mounted at the highest points of the roof, heavy conductors that run down the outside of the building, and grounding electrodes buried in the soil. When lightning strikes a rod, the current follows the conductors down and spreads out into the earth instead of passing through wood, brick or wiring, where it can start fires or crack walls. Because the current must reach the ground quickly, installers avoid sharp bends in the down conductors. A tight bend can make the current jump across the air to nearby metal.
>
> The idea dates to the 1750s, when Benjamin Franklin proposed iron rods for houses and church steeples. Early critics argued that the rods invited danger, and some church authorities resisted them for years. Over time, records of steeples that survived storms after rods were fitted helped change minds.

**Item 1.** According to the passage, what does a lightning rod do?

A. It draws lightning toward the building from far away · B. It stops lightning from striking the building · C. It gives a strike that is going to hit the building a path to the ground · D. It stores the electrical charge from a strike

**Item 2.** Why do installers avoid sharp bends in the down conductors?

A. Sharp bends make the rods attract more lightning · B. Sharp bends are harder to bury in the soil · C. Sharp bends make the conductors heavier · D. Sharp bends can make the current jump to nearby metal

**Item 3.** As it is used in the passage, the word "resisted" most nearly means:

A. supported · B. opposed · C. repaired · D. measured

**Item 4.** Which statement is best supported by the passage?

A. Evidence from real storms helped persuade people who doubted lightning rods · B. Franklin's idea was accepted at once by church authorities · C. Modern lightning rods are always made of iron · D. Lightning rods are no longer fitted to buildings

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 4</summary>

**Item 1: C.** "Its job is to give a strike that is already going to hit a building an easy path to the ground." *Why not A and B:* the first sentence says the opposite of both. These choices match what many people believe, which is why they are there.

**Item 2: D.** "A tight bend can make the current jump across the air to nearby metal." *Why not A:* the passage says rods do not attract lightning at all.

**Item 3: B.** Put each choice in place of the word: "some church authorities opposed them for years" keeps the meaning. The sentence is about critics who "argued that the rods invited danger," so the authorities were against the rods. *Why not A:* supported is the opposite.

**Item 4: A.** "Records of steeples that survived storms after rods were fitted helped change minds." That is evidence from real storms persuading doubters. *Why not B:* the passage says some church authorities "resisted them for years," so the idea was not accepted at once. *Why not C:* Franklin proposed iron rods, but the passage says nothing about what modern rods are made of, and "always" makes the claim impossible to support.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | C | D | B | A |

- **4 of 4:** you read carefully. Keep it sharp with timed passages, because the real ones are longer.
- **3 of 4:** check which type you missed. Most misses are inference or rules-with-exceptions. Practise pointing to the exact line before you choose.
- **2 or fewer:** slow down first, then speed up. Do two passages a day untimed, writing the line number next to every answer, until it is a habit.
